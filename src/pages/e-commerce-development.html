The Kenyan Government, through the Communications Authority of Kenya, is committed to facilitating the development of the information and communications sector, including broadcasting, multimedia, telecommunications and postal services, and electronic commerce.

Under CAP 411A of the laws of Kenya, the government is committed to facilitating the growth of electronic commerce (e-commerce) in the country. Put simply, the aim is to make it easier for Kenyans to trade online, locally or internationally.

What e-commerce is

Electronic commerce, or e-commerce, is trading, or facilitating trade, in products or services using computer networks such as the internet. It may be domestic, when it takes place within a country’s borders, or cross-border, when it crosses international borders. The key word is “trade”.

Ministries involved

It could be argued that every ministry has an interest in the growth of trade, but those with a direct stake are the ministries of Information and Communications Technology; Industrialisation; Devolution; Foreign Affairs and International Trade; Tourism; the East African Community; Labour and Social Protection; Mining; and Health. Each is a stakeholder in trade, and they need to work together where their interests overlap. E-commerce is one such area.

Background

E-commerce in Kenya is more common than most people realise. The 2016 National ICT Survey, carried out jointly by the Authority and the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS), found that 39% of private enterprises are engaged in e-commerce. Although the survey covered only private enterprises, not households, it was the first time e-commerce penetration could be measured at national level.

The internet is the e-commerce marketplace: buyers and sellers meet, and goods and services are exchanged. Payment may be made online or separately through mobile money or banking networks, which also rely on electronic data networks. Without an efficient internet and data network, e-commerce would not exist.

The Authority has long facilitated the growth of e-commerce. Penetration would not be where it is today without a stable telecommunications licensing environment, in which multiple data carriers offer services at relatively competitive rates, leading to widespread internet access.

The potential for e-commerce

Most Kenyans could potentially take part in e-commerce, depending on factors such as purchasing power, the availability of suitable products online, awareness of the opportunities, literacy levels and confidence in online trading platforms.

No data yet shows which of these factors hold back the growth of e-commerce, or to what extent. That makes it very difficult to develop policies to promote e-commerce, for example whether the country should focus on domestic e-commerce or on international e-commerce, which could earn foreign exchange.

Consumer protection online

Buying and selling online is also not yet easy for Kenyans. Unlike some countries where consumers’ rights online are explicitly protected, Kenya has not enacted such laws. Online trading platforms are not regulated under the Kenya Information and Communications Act (KICA), as they are not electronic services as envisaged under the Act and so cannot be licensed. Consumers therefore cannot rely on the Consumer Protection Regulations, 2010, which apply where the Authority’s licensees provide services.

Other jurisdictions have specific rules to protect consumers online. In the United Kingdom, for example, the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations, 2013, operationalised under the Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy, do this. Similar regulations need to be enacted in Kenya to build consumer confidence in online transactions and further develop e-commerce.

KICA does provide for the development of online payment platforms, through the protection given to consumers in Part VI (A) of the Act on electronic transactions and cybersecurity. Its sections provide a secure environment in which consumers can make online payments knowing they are protected by law. Online payment of taxes is one example, which has greatly eased cross-border trade: the Kenya Revenue Authority, which tracks tax payments and the entry and movement of transit goods, has deployed computer systems across the country, improving revenue assurance and making it easier to do business with neighbouring countries.

Couriers, addressing and skills

The Authority has also put protective mechanisms in place for the courier industry, which delivers items bought online. E-commerce guidelines for courier operators aim to ensure high levels of professionalism in conveying customers’ items, requiring couriers to keep consumer packages secure and to provide track-and-trace services.

Good progress has been made towards a National Addressing System for the country. The Authority has led this work by creating and funding a task force. A well-designed system will help e-commerce grow by making it easier to deliver directly to consumers.

To build capacity, the Authority has funded broadband connectivity for public secondary schools, which will improve literacy and introduce more people to trading online. This programme should be encouraged for its long-term benefits.

Global efforts

The Universal Postal Union (UPU) has introduced e-commerce hubs, in which the public postal operator in certain countries sets up a facility to act as a regional clearing house for international packages. The facility includes a warehouse, a sorting and dispatch centre and customs services, and packages are then distributed through the public postal operator’s network. Hubs have been initiated in South Africa, to serve Southern Africa, and Tunisia, to serve North Africa. The UPU has started discussions with Kenya on an East African hub, and further engagement is needed nationally and regionally to decide whether the concept suits the region.
